Hidden Deadly Dangers in Everyday Life

Hello. I work as a bioprinting researcher. In one of my recent studies, I ran into the problem of toxicity in the creation of biomaterials. While studying this issue, I realized that many of these problems also occur in everyday life. Realizing just how great a danger they represent, I decided to try to structure them into a single system. What you are about to read is common knowledge — there is nothing scientifically new in it. However, I tried to compactly fit it all into a single "puzzle" so that it would be easier to keep each of these factors constantly in mind.

In order not to cause unnecessary panic with specific examples, I call them "Factors with a non-zero probability of a delayed lethal effect." It sounds frightening, of course, but in my opinion, specifics would be far more frightening given how often these factors are present in everyday life. What is even more frightening is that it is extremely difficult to protect yourself from most of them, including aerosols. I would divide these factors into several conditional groups:

  • A) Harmful organic compounds;
  • B) Harmful external impacts;
  • C) Harmful additives.

Let's start with the first one.

A. Harmful organic compounds

Harmful organic compounds are those that are formed from organic objects (or are simply organic themselves). There is an enormous number of organic objects in our lives, so in my opinion, it is more convenient to go the other way around and compile a list of what is inorganic (of course, this is not a complete list, but rather what, in my opinion, we encounter in life in one way or another). This list includes: metals, pure non-metals, simple substances, glass, ceramics, water, oxygen, nitrogen, and inorganic minerals.

These harmful organic compounds manifest themselves mainly in the form of volatile organic compounds (VOCs), aldehydes, and toxic amides (acrylamide), as well as in the form of polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs). Among the reactions that form them, I would highlight the heating of organic matter, which triggers or intensifies the Maillard reaction, oxidation, and pyrolysis.

The first one, the Maillard reaction, is the reaction between amine and carbonyl groups — or in simple words, between proteins and sugars (including fructose). Such a reaction is present, for example, in fried or baked food with a high starch content. It can be observed in the form of a crust. Chips are dangerous because of this reaction (but not exclusively, as you understand). The second reaction is oxidation. Regarding oxidation, one must remember that atmospheric oxygen itself is an oxidizer. But it is not the only one — aggressive cleaning agents (like chlorine bleach) are as well. And finally, pyrolysis, the products of combustion — this is the very process which also contributes to the appearance of PAHs. The most vivid example is the embers on a barbecue. Again, it is not only the above-listed reactions in everyday life that produce these toxins. Some technical processes, for example SLA printing, also emit them. At the same time, these toxins are also contained within the composition of daily products — for example, one of the most toxic among aldehydes, formaldehyde, is contained in wood-based materials (particleboard). Varnishes, paints, and glues also frequently contain harmful VOCs, but that belongs more to another category.

B. Harmful external impacts

In this category, I would include ultraviolet radiation (UV), ozone, and ionizing radiation. UV is dangerous in itself, and in addition, a certain UV range generates ozone. The source of UV is mainly the sun (prolonged exposure to direct, intense sunlight is dangerous), quartz lamp treatment, manicure lamps, and tanning beds. Radiation is more complicated as it is very widespread. This includes trips to high mountains and, correspondingly, air travel as well. Even in basements, it is present in the form of radon.

C. Harmful additives

Among these, I would separately single out structural additives. These are nickel, vanadium (found in titanium alloys), cobalt, fiberglass, asbestos (in old building materials), and chromium. Chromium by itself, in its stable form, is safe. But upon oxidation or electrochemical reactions (for example, electrochemical polishing), it takes on an unstable form and becomes dangerous. As I already said, oxygen in the air is an oxidizer. Combined with heat, a catalyst of oxidation, machine-tool work (like drilling or grinding) without sufficient cooling can easily become a factor in the formation of its unstable form. And chromium, at that, is used to alloy if not the overwhelming majority, then simply the majority of metals.

Speaking of cooling — here I will return to the first factor. Cooling agents, oils, thermal pastes, and coolant fluids with a high degree of probability contain organic components within themselves. Yes, each organic component has its own degree of heat resistance in this sense; however, this does not negate the fact that they are used exclusively for contact with a heated object.

Now to other additives. There exists a number of harmful additives that are added to various finished products. Here, in my opinion, it is easier to simply list these additives so that in the future, one can look at a product's composition before purchase: nitrosamines, heavy metals, polychlorinated biphenyls, bisphenol A, phthalates, and brominated flame retardants. Nitrosamines, by the way, are sometimes added even to food.

This is where my list ends. Needless to say, the list of specific examples in this text is far from complete. However, if one constantly keeps in mind the nature and source of each of these impacts, one can learn to 'see' these hazards around. This will enhance your chances of seeing the day when biomedical technologies have advanced far ahead, and bioprinting becomes that which people wish to see it be. Therefore, perceive this text not as a 'horror story', but as 'safeguard information' and a source of hope — with the due desire and effort, any of these factors can be entirely avoided.

Long and happy years, dear reader!
